Module 01

Differentiability

Understand when a derivative exists and why continuity is necessary but not sufficient.

01. The derivative at a point

The derivative is the limit of the average rate of change. At an interior point a, a finite, two-sided limit must exist: f′(a) = limh→0 [f(a+h) − f(a)] / h. The left and right derivatives must agree. Differentiability at a implies continuity at a, but continuity alone does not guarantee differentiability.

Worked example

For f(x) = x², calculate f′(3) from the definition.

  1. [f(3+h) − f(3)]/h = [(3+h)² − 9]/h.
  2. Expand to obtain (6h+h²)/h = 6+h for h ≠ 0.
  3. Take the limit as h approaches 0: f′(3) = 6.

Interpretation: at x = 3, the tangent slope is 6.

02. Continuous does not always mean differentiable

A corner has unequal one-sided slopes; a cusp or vertical tangent can have an undefined or infinite slope. A discontinuity also prevents differentiability.

Worked example

Check f(x) = |x − 2| at x = 2.

  1. The function is continuous: f(2) = 0 and both one-sided limits equal 0.
  2. For x < 2, f(x) = 2−x, so the left derivative is −1.
  3. For x > 2, f(x) = x−2, so the right derivative is +1.
  4. The one-sided derivatives disagree; therefore f′(2) does not exist.

A continuous corner is a counterexample to the converse statement.

03. Cusps and vertical tangents

At a cusp and at a vertical tangent the curve is continuous, but the difference quotient grows without bound as h→0, so no finite derivative exists. At a cusp the one-sided slopes head to opposite infinities. At a vertical tangent they head to the same infinity.

Worked example

Test f(x) = x2/3 and g(x) = x1/3 at x = 0.

  1. For f: [f(h) − f(0)]/h = h2/3/h = 1/h1/3.
  2. As h→0⁺ this tends to +∞, and as h→0⁻ it tends to −∞. The slopes disagree, so f has a cusp and f′(0) does not exist.
  3. For g: [g(h) − g(0)]/h = h1/3/h = 1/h2/3.
  4. As h→0 from either side this tends to +∞. The tangent is the vertical line x = 0, so g has a vertical tangent and g′(0) does not exist.

The derivative formulas agree: f′(x) = (2/3)x−1/3 and g′(x) = (1/3)x−2/3 are both undefined at x = 0.

04. Piecewise functions

At a joining point, first match function values (continuity), then match one-sided derivatives (smoothness). Checking slopes alone is not enough.

Worked example

Let f(x) = x² for x ≤ 1 and f(x) = mx+b for x > 1. Find m and b for differentiability at 1.

  1. Continuity requires m+b = 1, because the left value equals 1.
  2. The left derivative at 1 is 2; the right derivative is m. Set m = 2.
  3. Then 2+b = 1, so b = −1.
  4. Both continuity and one-sided derivative conditions hold, so f is differentiable at 1.

Always test continuity before differentiability.

Module 02

Velocity & Acceleration

Connect derivatives to one-dimensional motion, direction, speed, and acceleration.

01. Position, velocity, acceleration

For a position function s(t), velocity is v(t) = s′(t) and acceleration is a(t) = v′(t) = s″(t). Position is measured in metres (m), velocity in m/s and acceleration in m/s². Speed is |v(t)|, not v(t).

Worked example

A particle has s(t)=t³−6t²+9t for 0 ≤ t ≤ 5.

  1. Differentiate: v(t)=3t²−12t+9=3(t−1)(t−3).
  2. Differentiate again: a(t)=6t−12=6(t−2).
  3. The particle is at rest when v=0, so t = 1 and t = 3 seconds.

Positive velocity means motion to the right; negative velocity means motion to the left.

02. Speeding up versus slowing down

Speed increases when velocity and acceleration have the same sign, v·a > 0; it decreases when signs differ, v·a < 0. At zeros of v or a, discuss nearby intervals rather than declaring the object faster or slower at that instant.

Worked example

Classify the particle above on (0,5).

  1. Split the time domain at t=1, 2 and 3.
  2. On (0,1): v>0 and a<0 → slowing down.
  3. On (1,2): v<0 and a<0 → speeding up.
  4. On (2,3): v<0 and a>0 → slowing down.
  5. On (3,5): v>0 and a>0 → speeding up.

Acceleration does not always mean “speeding up”. Its direction matters relative to velocity.

03. Displacement versus total distance

Displacement is final position minus initial position. Total distance travelled adds the absolute changes in position between turning times when velocity is zero.

Worked example

Find the displacement and total distance for the same particle on [0,5].

  1. Position values: s(0)=0; s(1)=4; s(3)=0; s(5)=20.
  2. Displacement = s(5)−s(0) = 20 m.
  3. Total distance = |4−0| + |0−4| + |20−0| = 28 m.

Distance is nonnegative and never smaller than the magnitude of displacement.

Module 03

Maxima, Minima & Extreme Values

Find and justify local and absolute extremes using derivative tests and endpoints.

01. Critical numbers and the EVT

A critical number is an interior domain value where f′(x)=0 or f′(x) does not exist. The Extreme Value Theorem says that a function continuous on the closed interval [a,b] attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum there. Candidates include critical numbers and endpoints.

Worked example

Find absolute extrema of f(x)=x³−3x on [−2,2].

  1. Compute f′(x)=3x²−3=3(x−1)(x+1).
  2. Critical numbers inside [−2,2]: x=−1 and x=1.
  3. Evaluate: f(−2)=−2, f(−1)=2, f(1)=−2, f(2)=2.
  4. Absolute maximum: 2 at x=−1 and x=2. Absolute minimum: −2 at x=−2 and x=1.

Do not forget the endpoints in a closed-interval problem.

02. First and second derivative tests

For the first derivative test, inspect the sign of f′ before and after a critical number: + to − means a local maximum; − to + means a local minimum. If f′(c)=0, a positive f″(c) means local minimum; negative f″(c) means local maximum. If f″(c)=0, the test is inconclusive.

Worked example

Classify the interior critical numbers of f(x)=x³−3x with both tests.

  1. First derivative test. f′(x)=3(x−1)(x+1), so check the sign of f′ on each side of x=−1 and x=1.

  1. f′ changes from + to − at x=−1, so there is a local maximum. It changes from − to + at x=1, so there is a local minimum.
  2. Second derivative test. f″(x)=6x. At x=−1, f″(−1)=−6<0, so there is a local maximum.
  3. At x=1, f″(1)=6>0, so there is a local minimum. Both tests agree.

A stationary point need not be a maximum or minimum: f(x)=x³ at x=0 is a counterexample.

03. A reliable optimization checklist

Use: (1) define the domain, (2) obtain a one-variable function, (3) differentiate, (4) identify critical points and endpoints, (5) justify and interpret the result. A derivative of zero identifies a candidate, not proof of a maximum.

Worked example

Consider g(x)=x² on [−2,3].

  1. g′(x)=2x=0 gives x=0.
  2. Evaluate: g(−2)=4, g(0)=0, g(3)=9.
  3. The absolute minimum is 0 at x=0; the absolute maximum is 9 at x=3.

Comparing candidate values confirms absolute extrema on a continuous closed interval.

Module 04

Optimization: Three Real-World Fields

Model a realistic objective, impose constraints, solve and explain the meaning of the optimum.

01. Design: open-top box

Required field: design. A 20 cm × 20 cm square sheet has identical corner squares removed, then folds into an open-top box. The cut size x must satisfy 0 < x < 10. We maximize volume, not surface area.

Worked example

Find the corner-cut size with greatest volume.

  1. Base side length = 20−2x; height = x. Hence V(x)=x(20−2x)².
  2. V′(x)=(20−2x)(20−6x).
  3. Set V′=0: x=10 (boundary/degenerate box) or x=10/3 (interior).
  4. V approaches 0 at each boundary; volume is positive inside. Thus x=10/3 cm ≈ 3.33 cm maximizes volume.
  5. Vmax = (10/3)(40/3)² = 16000/27 cm³ ≈ 592.59 cm³.

The domain and boundary behavior are part of the justification.

02. Economics: maximum profit

Required field: economics. In a simplified demand model, price is p(q)=100−2q dollars per item, fixed and variable costs are C(q)=200+20q dollars. Demand requires 0 ≤ q ≤ 50. Profit is revenue minus cost.

Worked example

Choose production q that maximizes profit.

  1. R(q)=q·p(q)=100q−2q².
  2. P(q)=R(q)−C(q)=80q−2q²−200.
  3. P′(q)=80−4q; solve P′=0 → q=20. Equivalently, marginal revenue R′(q)=100−4q equals marginal cost C′(q)=20 at q=20.
  4. P″(q)=−4<0, so the profit curve is concave down on [0,50] and q=20 gives the absolute maximum.
  5. Maximum profit: P(20)=$600, at 20 items.

MR=MC is a necessary interior stationary condition, not automatic proof of maximum profit.

03. Biological science: enzyme activity

Required field: biological science. Model enzyme activity in a hypothetical classroom experiment as A(T)=10−0.02(T−35)², where T is temperature (°C), 20 ≤ T ≤ 50 and A is an activity index (arbitrary units). This is a pedagogical model, not measured biological data.

Worked example

At what temperature is predicted activity greatest?

  1. A′(T)=−0.04(T−35).
  2. Set A′=0 to obtain T=35°C.
  3. A″(T)=−0.04<0 and 35 lies in the allowed interval.
  4. The model predicts maximum A(35)=10 activity units at 35°C.

Model assumptions and validity ranges matter when interpreting applications.

04. Biological science: drug concentration

A second biology example. After a dose, a drug is absorbed into the bloodstream and then cleared. A simplified model is C(t) = 10t/(t²+4), where C is the concentration (mg/L) and t ≥ 0 is the time in hours after the dose. The numbers are illustrative, not medical data.

Worked example

When does the concentration peak, and what is the peak?

  1. Quotient rule: C′(t) = [10(t²+4) − 10t(2t)]/(t²+4)² = 10(4−t²)/(t²+4)².
  2. Set C′=0: 4−t²=0, so t=2. The value t=−2 is outside the domain.
  3. C′>0 for 0≤t<2 and C′<0 for t>2, so by the first derivative test t=2 is a maximum.
  4. Peak: C(2) = 20/8 = 2.5 mg/L, 2 hours after the dose.

C rises, peaks once, then falls toward 0, so this local maximum is also the absolute maximum on t ≥ 0.

HW 1 · Differentiability

5 questions · solutions provided

1.1 Determine whether f(x)=|x+1| is differentiable at x=−1. Explain with one-sided derivatives.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. For x<−1, f(x)=−x−1, so the left derivative is −1.
  2. For x>−1, f(x)=x+1, so the right derivative is +1.
  3. Both pieces meet continuously at f(−1)=0, but slopes differ. Therefore the function is not differentiable at x=−1.

1.2 Find a and b such that f(x)=x²+1 for x≤2 and f(x)=ax+b for x>2 is differentiable at x=2.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Continuity: 2a+b = f(2)=2²+1=5.
  2. Left derivative: d(x²+1)/dx at x=2 equals 4. Right derivative equals a.
  3. Set a=4, then 8+b=5 gives b=−3.

1.3 Is f(x)=3x−1 for x<1 and f(x)=x²+1 for x≥1 differentiable at x=1?

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Left limit 3(1)−1=2; right limit and function value 1²+1=2. Hence continuous.
  2. Left derivative is 3. Right derivative at 1 is 2(1)=2.
  3. Different one-sided derivatives mean f′(1) does not exist.

1.4 Show from the limit definition that f(x)=x² has derivative 2x at x=a.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. [f(a+h)−f(a)]/h = [(a+h)²−a²]/h.
  2. Simplify to [2ah+h²]/h = 2a+h for h ≠ 0.
  3. As h→0 the limit is 2a, so f′(a)=2a.

1.5 Use the limit definition to show that f(x)=x4/5 is not differentiable at x=0. Does the graph have a cusp or a vertical tangent there?

Show step-by-step solution
  1. f is continuous at x=0, because h4/5→0=f(0) as h→0.
  2. The difference quotient is [f(h)−f(0)]/h = h4/5/h = 1/h1/5.
  3. As h→0⁺ this tends to +∞, and as h→0⁻ it tends to −∞.
  4. The one-sided slopes head to opposite infinities, so f′(0) does not exist and the graph has a cusp at x=0.

HW 2 · Velocity & Acceleration

4 questions · solutions provided

2.1 For s(t)=2t³−9t²+12t (metres), find v(t), a(t), v(3), and a(3).

Show step-by-step solution
  1. v(t)=s′(t)=6t²−18t+12.
  2. a(t)=v′(t)=12t−18.
  3. v(3)=54−54+12=12 m/s; a(3)=36−18=18 m/s².

2.2 For v(t)=t²−5t+6 on 0≤t≤4, find when the particle is at rest and the intervals where it is speeding up.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Factor v(t)=(t−2)(t−3), so the particle is at rest at t=2 and t=3.
  2. a(t)=v′(t)=2t−5, so a=0 at t=2.5.
  3. On (2,2.5), v<0 and a<0. On (3,4), v>0 and a>0. The signs match, so these are the speeding-up intervals.
  4. On (0,2) and (2.5,3), v and a have opposite signs, so the particle is slowing down there.

2.3 For s(t)=t²−4t on [0,5], calculate displacement and total distance.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. v(t)=2t−4=0 at t=2.
  2. s(0)=0; s(2)=−4; s(5)=5.
  3. Displacement=5−0=5 m; total distance=|−4−0|+|5−(−4)|=4+9=13 m.

2.4 Explain why an object with v=−4 m/s and a=−2 m/s² is speeding up at that instant.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Velocity is negative, so motion is in the negative direction.
  2. Acceleration is also negative, so it increases the magnitude of negative velocity.
  3. Because v·a=8>0, its speed is increasing.

HW 3 · Maxima & Minima

5 questions · solutions provided

3.1 Find absolute extrema of f(x)=x²−6x+8 on [0,5].

Show step-by-step solution
  1. f′(x)=2x−6=0 at x=3.
  2. f(0)=8; f(3)=9−18+8=−1; f(5)=25−30+8=3.
  3. Absolute max=8 at x=0; absolute min=−1 at x=3.

3.2 Find the absolute extrema of f(x)=2x³−3x²−12x+1 on [−2,3].

Show step-by-step solution
  1. f′(x)=6x²−6x−12=6(x−2)(x+1), so the critical numbers are x=−1 and x=2. Both are inside the interval.
  2. Evaluate: f(−2)=−3; f(−1)=8; f(2)=−19; f(3)=−8.
  3. Absolute maximum=8 at x=−1. Absolute minimum=−19 at x=2.

3.3 Classify the stationary points of f(x)=x⁴−4x² on ℝ.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. f′(x)=4x³−8x=4x(x²−2). Critical points x=0, ±√2.
  2. f″(x)=12x²−8. At 0 it is −8<0, so 0 is a local maximum with f(0)=0.
  3. At ±√2 it equals 16>0, so these are local minima, each with value (√2)⁴−4(√2)²=4−8=−4.

3.4 Does f(x)=x for 0≤x<1 and f(1)=0 have an absolute maximum on [0,1]? Explain EVT.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. For any x<1, there is another y between x and 1 with f(y)=y>x.
  2. At x=1 the function equals 0. Therefore it never reaches its supremum 1 and has no maximum.
  3. The function is discontinuous at 1, so EVT does not apply.

3.5 Use the first derivative test to classify the critical numbers of f(x)=x⁴−4x³. Why would the second derivative test not settle x=0?

Show step-by-step solution
  1. f′(x)=4x³−12x²=4x²(x−3), so the critical numbers are x=0 and x=3.
  2. Test the sign of f′: f′(−1)=−16<0; f′(1)=−8<0; f′(4)=64>0.
  3. At x=0, f′ stays negative on both sides, so x=0 is neither a maximum nor a minimum.
  4. At x=3, f′ changes from − to +, so x=3 is a local minimum, with f(3)=81−108=−27.
  5. f″(x)=12x²−24x gives f″(0)=0, so the second derivative test is inconclusive at x=0. The first derivative test still gives an answer.

HW 4 · Optimization Applications

4 questions · solutions provided

4.1 A 24 cm × 9 cm rectangular sheet becomes an open-top box by cutting a square of side x from each corner. Find the x that maximizes the volume, and state that volume.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. The base is (24−2x) by (9−2x) and the height is x, so V(x)=x(24−2x)(9−2x)=4x³−66x²+216x, with 0<x<4.5.
  2. V′(x)=12x²−132x+216=12(x−2)(x−9). The value x=9 is outside the domain, so x=2 is the only candidate.
  3. V″(x)=24x−132 and V″(2)=−84<0, so x=2 gives a maximum.
  4. V(2)=2(20)(5)=200 cm³, reached by cutting 2 cm squares.

4.2 A product has price p(q)=80−q and cost C(q)=100+20q, 0≤q≤80. Maximize profit.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Revenue: R(q)=q·p(q)=80q−q².
  2. Profit: P(q)=R(q)−C(q)=60q−q²−100.
  3. P′(q)=60−2q=0 gives q=30, which is inside [0,80].
  4. P″(q)=−2<0, so q=30 is a maximum. The endpoints are lower: P(0)=−100 and P(80)=−1700.
  5. Maximum profit: P(30)=1800−900−100=$800, at 30 units.

4.3 A simplified model for the energy a migrating bird uses per kilometre at speed v (m/s) is E(v)=0.05v²+100/v, for 2≤v≤25. Find the speed that uses the least energy, and explain why flying very slowly is not efficient.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. E′(v)=0.1v−100/v².
  2. Set E′=0: 0.1v³=100, so v³=1000 and v=10 m/s.
  3. E″(v)=0.1+200/v³>0, so v=10 is a minimum.
  4. Compare the endpoints: E(2)=50.2; E(10)=15; E(25)=35.25. The least energy is 15 units per km at 10 m/s.
  5. At low speed the 100/v term becomes large: the trip takes longer, so each kilometre costs more energy. This is a classroom model with arbitrary units.

4.4 A garden against a straight wall requires 80 m of fencing for only three sides. Find maximum area.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Let x be each perpendicular side and y the parallel fenced side: 2x+y=80.
  2. Area A=xy=x(80−2x), 0≤x≤40.
  3. A′=80−4x=0 → x=20; then y=40. A″=−4<0.
  4. The maximum area is 20·40=800 m².

Assessment 1 · Differentiability & Motion

5 questions · 18 marks · K3 / A8 / T4 / C3

K / 3 Q1. State the relationship between differentiability and continuity. Give a counterexample to the converse.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. If f is differentiable at x=a, then f is continuous at x=a.
  2. The converse is false: a continuous function does not have to be differentiable.
  3. Counterexample: f(x)=|x| is continuous at x=0, but its one-sided derivatives are −1 and +1, so f′(0) does not exist.

A / 4 Q2. Let f(x)=x²−3x for x≤2 and f(x)=mx+b for x>2. Find m and b so that f is differentiable at x=2.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Continuity: f(2)=4−6=−2, so 2m+b=−2.
  2. Left derivative: 2x−3 at x=2 equals 1. Right derivative: m. Therefore m=1.
  3. Substitute: 2(1)+b=−2, so b=−4.
  4. With m=1 and b=−4, the values and the slopes match, so f is differentiable at x=2.

A / 4 Q3. A particle has position s(t)=t³−3t² (s in metres, t in seconds, t≥0). Find its velocity and acceleration, then describe its motion at t=1.5.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. v(t)=3t²−6t=3t(t−2), in m/s.
  2. a(t)=6t−6, in m/s².
  3. v(1.5)=3(1.5)(−0.5)=−2.25 m/s and a(1.5)=3 m/s².
  4. The particle is moving in the negative direction. v and a have opposite signs, so it is slowing down.

T / 4 Q4. Generalize Q3. For s(t)=t³−kt² with k>0 and t≥0, find in terms of k the interval on which the particle slows down. Then find the value of k that makes this interval (2,4).

Show step-by-step solution
  1. v(t)=3t²−2kt=t(3t−2k), so v=0 at t=0 and t=2k/3.
  2. a(t)=6t−2k, so a=0 at t=k/3.
  3. On (0,k/3), v<0 and a<0: speeding up. On (k/3,2k/3), v<0 and a>0: slowing down. For t>2k/3, v>0 and a>0: speeding up.
  4. The particle slows down on (k/3,2k/3). Setting k/3=2 gives k=6, and then 2k/3=4 as required.

C / 3 Q5. A classmate writes: “In Q3 the acceleration at t=1.5 is positive, so the particle must be speeding up.” Explain the error in words.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Positive acceleration means the velocity is increasing, which is not the same as the speed increasing.
  2. At t=1.5 the velocity is −2.25 m/s, so the particle moves in the negative direction while the acceleration acts in the positive direction. The velocity becomes less negative, so the speed |v| gets smaller.
  3. The correct test compares signs: v·a=(−2.25)(3)<0, so the particle is slowing down.

Assessment 2 · Extreme Values

4 questions · 14 marks · K2 / A5 / T4 / C3

K / 2 Q1. Define a critical number of f on its domain.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. A number c in the interior of the domain of f is a critical number if f′(c)=0 or f′(c) does not exist.

A / 5 Q2. Find the absolute extrema of f(x)=x²−4x+1 on [−1,4].

Show step-by-step solution
  1. f′(x)=2x−4=0 at x=2, which is inside [−1,4].
  2. Evaluate the candidates: f(−1)=6; f(2)=−3; f(4)=1.
  3. f is continuous on a closed interval, so the largest and smallest values are the absolute extrema: maximum 6 at x=−1, minimum −3 at x=2.

T / 4 Q3. Find a and b so that f(x)=x³+ax²+bx has a local maximum at x=−1 and a local minimum at x=3. Verify your answer.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. f′(x)=3x²+2ax+b, and both x=−1 and x=3 must be zeros of f′.
  2. So f′(x)=3(x+1)(x−3)=3x²−6x−9.
  3. Compare coefficients: 2a=−6 gives a=−3, and b=−9.
  4. Verify with f″(x)=6x−6: f″(−1)=−12<0 gives a local maximum, and f″(3)=12>0 gives a local minimum.

C / 3 Q4. State the Extreme Value Theorem and explain why continuity and a closed interval both matter.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. EVT: if f is continuous on a closed interval [a,b], then f attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on [a,b].
  2. Continuity matters because a jump can skip the highest value: f(x)=x for 0≤x<1 with f(1)=0 has no maximum on [0,1].
  3. A closed interval matters because an open one can leave out the extreme value: f(x)=x on (0,1) gets close to 1 but never reaches it.

Assessment 3 · Applied Optimization

5 questions · 20 marks · K3 / A10 / T4 / C3

K / 3 Q1. In an optimization problem, define (a) the objective function and (b) a constraint. Then state what must be checked after solving f′(x)=0.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. (a) The objective function is the quantity being maximized or minimized.
  2. (b) A constraint is a condition that limits the variables. It reduces the problem to one variable and sets the domain.
  3. After solving f′(x)=0, justify the result with a derivative test and compare the endpoints of the domain.

A / 5 Q2. Design. A closed cylindrical can must hold 500 cm³. Find the radius and height that use the least material.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Constraint: πr²h=500, so h=500/(πr²), with r>0.
  2. Objective: S(r)=2πr²+2πrh=2πr²+1000/r.
  3. S′(r)=4πr−1000/r²=0 gives r³=250/π, so r≈4.30 cm.
  4. S″(r)=4π+2000/r³>0 for r>0, so this is a minimum.
  5. h=500/(πr²)≈8.60 cm, which equals 2r. The least material is about 348.7 cm².

A / 5 Q3. Economics. A theatre sells 300 tickets at $20 each. A survey suggests that each $1 price reduction sells 25 more tickets. What price maximizes revenue?

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Let x be the number of $1 reductions, 0≤x≤20. Price is 20−x and tickets sold are 300+25x.
  2. R(x)=(20−x)(300+25x)=6000+200x−25x².
  3. R′(x)=200−50x=0 gives x=4.
  4. R″(x)=−50<0, so x=4 is a maximum. The endpoints are lower: R(0)=6000 and R(20)=0.
  5. Charge $16 to sell 400 tickets, for maximum revenue R(4)=$6400.

T / 4 Q4. Biology. During a cough the windpipe narrows. A classic simplified model for the air speed is v(r)=k(R−r)r², where R is the normal radius, r is the narrowed radius with R/2≤r≤R, and k>0 is a constant. Find the r that maximizes air speed and explain what it means.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. v(r)=k(Rr²−r³), so v′(r)=k(2Rr−3r²)=kr(2R−3r).
  2. v′=0 at r=0, which is outside the domain, and at r=2R/3.
  3. Compare the candidates: v(R/2)=kR³/8=0.125kR³; v(2R/3)=4kR³/27≈0.148kR³; v(R)=0.
  4. The maximum is at r=2R/3. In this model, air moves fastest when the windpipe narrows to about two-thirds of its normal radius, which helps to clear it.

C / 3 Q5. Explain, with an example, why solving P′(q)=0 alone does not guarantee maximum profit.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. P′(q)=0 only finds a stationary candidate, which could be a maximum, a minimum or neither.
  2. Example: P(q)=q³−6q²+9q on [0,5] has P′(q)=3(q−1)(q−3). At q=3 there is a local minimum, P(3)=0.
  3. Even q=1, a local maximum with P(1)=4, is not the absolute maximum, because the endpoint gives P(5)=20. So test the candidates and compare the endpoints.

Final Assessment · Unit Test

11 questions · 50 marks · K10 / A15 / T15 / C10

Suggested time: 60–75 minutes. Show mathematical working, correct units and written reasoning.

K / 4 Q1. For f(x)=|x|, determine differentiability at x=0 using one-sided derivatives.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. f is continuous at x=0, because |x|→0=f(0) as x→0.
  2. Left derivative: for h<0, [f(h)−f(0)]/h=|h|/h=−h/h=−1.
  3. Right derivative: for h>0, [f(h)−f(0)]/h=|h|/h=h/h=1.
  4. The one-sided derivatives differ, so f′(0) does not exist. f is continuous but not differentiable at x=0.

K / 3 Q2. State the definitions of velocity and acceleration in terms of position, including SI units.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Velocity is the rate of change of position: v(t)=s′(t), in m/s.
  2. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a(t)=v′(t)=s″(t), in m/s².
  3. Speed is |v(t)|. Velocity includes direction and speed does not.

K / 3 Q3. State the Extreme Value Theorem, including its hypotheses.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Hypotheses: f is continuous on a closed, bounded interval [a,b].
  2. Conclusion: f attains at least one absolute maximum and at least one absolute minimum on [a,b].
  3. These values occur at critical numbers or at the endpoints.

A / 5 Q4. Given f(x)=x²+2 for x≤1 and f(x)=mx+b for x>1, find m and b so that f is differentiable at x=1.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Continuity: f(1)=1²+2=3, so m+b=3.
  2. Left derivative: 2x at x=1 equals 2. Right derivative: m.
  3. Differentiability requires m=2.
  4. Substitute: 2+b=3, so b=1.
  5. Check: the right piece gives 2(1)+1=3 with slope 2, matching the left piece.

A / 5 Q5. For s(t)=t²−6t (s in metres, t in seconds) over 0≤t≤5, find the rest time, displacement and total distance.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. v(t)=2t−6, so the particle is at rest at t=3 s.
  2. Positions: s(0)=0, s(3)=−9, s(5)=−5.
  3. Displacement=s(5)−s(0)=−5 m.
  4. Total distance=|−9−0|+|−5−(−9)|=9+4=13 m.

A / 5 Q6. Find the absolute maximum and minimum of f(x)=x³−12x+5 on [−3,5].

Show step-by-step solution
  1. f′(x)=3x²−12=3(x−2)(x+2), so the critical numbers are x=−2 and x=2. Both are inside the interval.
  2. Evaluate: f(−3)=14; f(−2)=21; f(2)=−11; f(5)=70.
  3. Absolute maximum=70 at the endpoint x=5. Absolute minimum=−11 at x=2.

T / 5 Q7. Design. A rectangular poster must contain 384 cm² of printed text, with 6 cm margins at the top and bottom and 4 cm margins on each side. Find the poster dimensions that minimize its total area.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Let the printed region be w by h, in cm, with wh=384, so h=384/w and w>0.
  2. The poster is (w+8) by (h+12), so A(w)=(w+8)(384/w+12)=480+12w+3072/w.
  3. A′(w)=12−3072/w²=0 gives w²=256, so w=16.
  4. A″(w)=6144/w³>0, so this is a minimum.
  5. h=384/16=24. The poster is 24 cm wide and 36 cm tall, with area 864 cm².

T / 5 Q8. Economics. A firm faces p(q)=60−q and C(q)=120+10q for 0≤q≤60. (a) Find the profit-maximizing quantity and price. (b) A $10 per-item tax is added to its costs. How do the quantity, price and maximum profit change?

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Before the tax: P(q)=q(60−q)−(120+10q)=50q−q²−120.
  2. P′=50−2q=0 gives q=25, and P″=−2<0. The price is $35 and P(25)=1250−625−120=$505.
  3. With the tax, cost is 120+20q, so P(q)=40q−q²−120.
  4. P′=40−2q=0 gives q=20, and P″=−2<0. The price is $40 and P(20)=800−400−120=$280.
  5. Output falls by 5 items, the price rises by $5 and maximum profit falls by $225. The firm passes only half of the $10 tax on through its price.

T / 5 Q9. Biology. A simplified model for the energy a salmon uses to swim upstream against a 4 km/h current is E(v)=cv³/(v−4), where v>4 is its swimming speed in km/h and c>0 is a constant. Find the speed that minimizes energy and state one assumption of the model.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Quotient rule: E′(v)=c[3v²(v−4)−v³]/(v−4)²=cv²(2v−12)/(v−4)².
  2. E′=0 when 2v−12=0, so v=6 km/h. The value v=0 is outside the domain.
  3. E′<0 for 4<v<6 and E′>0 for v>6, so v=6 is a minimum.
  4. The salmon uses the least energy at 6 km/h, which is 1.5 times the speed of the current.
  5. One assumption: the current stays constant at 4 km/h for the whole trip.

C / 5 Q10. A student writes: “f′(a)=0 always means f has a local maximum or minimum at a, and every local maximum happens where f′(a)=0.” Correct both claims, giving an example for each.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. The first claim is false. f(x)=(x−1)³ has f′(1)=0, but f′(x)=3(x−1)²≥0 on both sides, so f keeps increasing and has no extremum at x=1.
  2. The second claim is false. f(x)=−|x| has a local maximum at x=0, but f′(0) does not exist.
  3. Correct statement: local extrema can occur only at critical numbers (f′=0 or f′ does not exist), and the sign of f′ must change for a critical number to be an extremum.

C / 5 Q11. A driver says, “My acceleration is negative, so I must be slowing down.” Explain in words when this is true and when it is false. Include signs and a numerical example of each case.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Negative acceleration means the velocity is decreasing. On its own it does not say whether the speed is decreasing.
  2. True case, v>0 and a<0: with v=10 m/s and a=−2 m/s², v·a=−20<0, so the car slows down.
  3. False case, v<0 and a<0: with v=−10 m/s and a=−2 m/s², v·a=20>0, so the car speeds up.
  4. The driver must compare the signs of v and a, not look at a alone.
